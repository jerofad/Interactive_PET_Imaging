Chapter 3 of 10

Photon detection

Each photon must be stopped in a scintillation crystal. The crystal turns deposited energy into light, and a photosensor such as a SiPM turns light into a pulse. That pulse gives the energy, arrival time and position of the photon.

Chapter 3 / Photon detection

A crystal turns deposited energy
into a measurable pulse.

In the crystal, a photon may be absorbed photoelectrically or may Compton-scatter. If its full energy is eventually contained, it contributes to the photopeak. If a scattered photon escapes, only part of its energy is deposited.

For a 511 keV photon, a single Compton interaction can transfer at most about 341 keV to an electron. The corresponding backscattered photon retains about 170 keV. These are different energies, not competing definitions of the same edge.

E′ = E / [1 + (E/mₑc²)(1 − cosθ)]
Compton edge = 511 − 511/3 keV
Energy resolution = FWHM / Epeak

Scintillation light is converted to charge by a photosensor such as a SiPM. Integrating the signal estimates deposited energy; timing electronics estimate photon arrival time. A scintillator’s light-decay time is not its coincidence timing resolution.

In a multiplexed block, Anger logic estimates position from charge-weighted sensor coordinates: x̂ = Σqᵢxᵢ/Σqᵢ and ŷ = Σqᵢyᵢ/Σqᵢ. A calibrated flood map assigns that estimate to a crystal.

Key takeaways

  • An energy window rejects many scattered photons, because scattering lowers photon energy.
  • Energy resolution limits how cleanly the photopeak separates from scatter.
  • A crystal’s light-decay time is not the same as the scanner’s coincidence timing resolution.
