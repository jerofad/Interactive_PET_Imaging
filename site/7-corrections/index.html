Chapter 7 of 10

Corrections

Raw coincidences are biased by detector efficiency, attenuation, scatter, randoms, dead time and decay. Quantitative PET builds each effect into the reconstruction model.

Chapter 7 / Attenuation

Both photons must survive
the entire line of response.

A photon that interacts before reaching the detector may no longer contribute to a true coincidence. For a uniform path, survival of the pair is the product of survival along the two opposing paths.

Ppair = exp(−μℓ₁) · exp(−μℓ₂) = exp(−μ(ℓ₁+ℓ₂))
General case: Ppair = exp(−∫LOR μ dl)
Attenuation correction factor = 1/Ppair

Move the annihilation point. One photon travels farther and the other travels less, but the total path through the object stays the same. The unscattered-pair survival therefore does not depend on where along that LOR annihilation occurs.

CT measures attenuation at X-ray energies. A calibrated, often bilinear transformation maps HU to μ at 511 keV. Its bone slope depends on the acquisition and calibration; it is not a universal conversion.

MRI measures a different physical signal and provides no direct 511 keV μ-map. Tissue segmentation, specialised MR sequences and learned pseudo-CT/μ-maps bridge the gap. For a learned method, verify bone, air cavities, implants and out-of-distribution anatomy; a plausible image can still give biased PET quantification. An example of learned brain MR attenuation correction.

Water μ and CT scaling: Burger et al. (2002) and Carney et al. (2006).

Chapter 7 / The other corrections

Every effect gets a model.

Attenuation is the largest correction. The others are smaller, but quantitative PET needs all of them.

Normalisation

Detector pairs differ in efficiency because of crystal quality, geometry and electronics. A scan of a uniform source measures these differences. Component-based methods split them into crystal efficiencies and geometric factors, so fewer counts are needed for a precise estimate.

Badawi & Marsden (1999). The labs use a known efficiency pattern instead of a measured one.

Scatter estimation

Scattered photons follow broken paths, so they add counts to the wrong lines. Single-scatter simulation computes the expected scatter from the current activity estimate and the attenuation map. It is then scaled to the measured counts in the sinogram tails outside the body.

Watson (2000). The labs subtract the exact simulated scatter, so their scatter correction is ideal.

Randoms estimation

A delayed coincidence window shifts one detector’s timestamps far beyond the prompt window, so only accidental pairs remain. Alternatively, randoms can be estimated from the singles rates with R = 2τS₁S₂. The delayed-window estimate carries its own Poisson noise, which is why NECR uses 2R.

IAEA, Quality Assurance for PET and PET/CT Systems (2009).

Dead time and decay

At high count rates, detectors and electronics miss events while they are busy. Dead-time correction scales the measured rate by a model of the losses. It restores the expected count level but not the statistics of the lost events.

Decay correction refers every frame to one reference time, as in Chapter 1.

PET/CT and PET/MR

In PET/CT, the CT image is converted to a 511 keV μ-map with a calibrated, usually bilinear mapping. PET and CT are acquired one after the other, so motion between them misaligns the map (see Chapter 10).

MRI does not measure photon attenuation, so PET/MR needs a substitute μ-map: tissue segmentation, atlases or learned pseudo-CT images (see the attenuation section above).

Kinahan et al. (1998) · Townsend (2008).

Key takeaways

  • Pair survival depends on the total path through the body along the line, not on where along it the annihilation happened.
  • Normalisation and attenuation multiply the expected counts. Scatter and randoms add to them.
  • Every correction is only as good as the model and the measurements behind it.
